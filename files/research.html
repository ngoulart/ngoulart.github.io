Heatwaves and Delivery Practices: The Effects of Extreme Temperature in Brazilian Maternity Wards
with Rafael Parfitt
Journal of Development Economics, 2026
Published Version
This paper examines how extreme heat affects healthcare delivery, using data on around 25 million births in Brazilian public hospitals between 2008 and 2019. Combining satellite-based temperature records with a high-dimensional fixed-effects design, we study how delivery practices and provider allocation vary with short-run temperature fluctuations in maternity wards. We find that extreme heat is associated with a lower probability that deliveries are led by physicians and a corresponding increase in nurse-led births, alongside a decline in cesarean section rates. Among physician-attended deliveries, extremely hot days are associated with a higher likelihood of pre-labor cesarean sections, consistent with shifts toward faster procedures under thermal stress. We find no detectable effects on immediate neonatal health as measured by Apgar scores. Together, these results suggest that temperature shocks reshape clinical decision-making and task allocation in maternity wards, revealing an operational channel through which climate stress affects healthcare delivery.

Sustaining Rules
with Emanuel Ornelas and Daniel Monte
CEPR CEP
We study the sustainability of international trading rules in a multipolar world. A rules-based equilibrium is shaped by three forces. A static temptation to exploit market power undermines cooperation, while two dynamic forces support it: the efficiency gains from rules and the cost of reestablishing the regime once a country becomes hegemonic. When multipolarity is short-lived and involves few co-leaders, a strong enough prospect of future hegemony ensures rules cooperation. However, in a more fragmented world, the sustainability of rules is more likely if shared leadership is expected to persist, to ensure long-lasting efficiency gains.
Randomization Inference under Spatiotemporal Correlation
with Bruno Ferman and Luis Alvarez
We develop theoretical foundations for temporal randomization inference in panel regressions with spatial dependence. Our framework establishes asymptotic validity for temporal permutation tests under stationarity and α-mixing, accommodating arbitrary spatial correlation while requiring no restrictions on the cross-sectional dimension. Standard permutation methods achieve correct size under ideal conditions but exhibit distortions when assumptions are violated; we propose studentization and a Frisch-Waugh-Lovell transformation to restore validity under time-varying variances and fixed effects. A doubly robust result shows that size control holds under either large-T stationarity or a large-(N,T) pivotality condition — ensuring validity even in non-stationary settings, as long as the studentized statistics share a common asymptotic distribution across time periods.
How does a fraud scandal impact trust in science?
with Leon Eliezer and Luigi Garzon
SSRN
This paper studies how unethical behavior in scientific research impacts trust in science. We run an information delivery experiment with high school students in Brazil, where we present them with a summary of a suspected fraud scandal in behavioral sciences. We divide treated subjects into a “fraud” arm, where they only learn about the fraud accusations, an “accountability” arm, where they learn about the investigation and punishment processes involved, and a control group that does not learn about the fraud scandal. This allows us to tell apart the effects of learning about cheating from the effects of learning about the accountability process. We find that treated students are less likely to update their personal beliefs according to scientific evidence and are less interested in science-related books. We find suggestive evidence that they are less willing to share scientific evidence. Results are driven by decreased trust in behavioral sciences, spilling over to authors who were not suspected of fraud.
Delaying Information and Dynamic Blackwell Dominance
with Henrique de Oliveira
We study how Blackwell’s informativeness ordering extends to dynamic, multi-period experiments. For two-period experiments, three natural notions of dominance arise: adapted garbling, which requires a non-anticipating simulation of one experiment from another; mean-preserving spreads of posterior processes, which demands that averaging the dominant experiment’s posteriors over the dominated experiment’s information recovers the latter’s posteriors at each period; and a submartingale coupling condition defined via convex functions. We show that adapted garbling implies mean-preserving spread dominance, and that the latter is equivalent to the submartingale coupling order.
Dump or Be Dumped: Anti-Dumping Policies in Dynamic General Equilibrium
with Michel Wachsmann
We develop a multi-country, multi-sector quantitative trade model to study the dynamic effects of anti-dumping policies. Building on the Arkolakis-Costinot-Rodriguez-Clare (2012) gravity framework, we embed an exogenous AD policy process taken from the World Bank Temporary Trade Barriers Database. Labor is immobile across sectors. Each period is a static equilibrium given the observed AD path. We estimate trade elasticities via IV and perform counterfactual exercises including AD removal, sector-specific duties, and retaliation cascades.
